Threats to Computer Systems

Computer systems face threats from malware, physical risks, and human actions like cracking. Understanding these threats is the first step in protecting systems and data.

Learning Objectives

  • 12.1.3.2 Analyze problems arising due to malware, physical risks
  • 12.1.3.1 Analyze ethical problems arising due to computer system cracking

Conceptual Anchor

The House Security Analogy

Your house faces threats: burglars (hackers), fire (hardware failure), termites (malware slowly eating from inside), and floods (power surges). Each threat requires a different protection strategy.

Rules & Theory

Types of Malware

Malware How It Works Damage
Virus Attaches to a program; spreads when the program runs Corrupts/deletes files, slows system
Worm Self-replicates across networks without user action Consumes bandwidth, crashes networks
Trojan Horse Disguised as legitimate software Creates backdoor for attacker access
Spyware Secretly monitors user activity Steals passwords, browsing history, personal data
Ransomware Encrypts files; demands payment for decryption key Data loss, financial damage
Adware Displays unwanted advertisements Slows system, tracks browsing habits
Keylogger Records every keystroke Captures passwords, credit card numbers

Physical Risks

Risk Description Prevention
Power surge / failure Sudden electrical spike or loss damages hardware UPS (Uninterruptible Power Supply), surge protectors
Fire / flood Natural disaster destroys equipment Off-site backups, fire suppression systems
Hardware failure Hard drive crash, component wear RAID, regular backups, maintenance
Theft Physical stealing of devices Locks, CCTV, encryption of storage
Environmental Overheating, humidity, dust Climate control, clean server rooms

Hacking vs Cracking — Ethical Issues

Term Definition Legal?
Hacker (white hat) Tests security with permission to find vulnerabilities Yes — ethical, legal
Cracker (black hat) Breaks into systems without permission for malicious purposes No — criminal offense

Ethical Problems from Cracking

  • Privacy violation — accessing personal data without consent
  • Financial damage — stealing funds, causing business losses
  • Identity theft — using stolen credentials to impersonate victims
  • National security — attacking government infrastructure
  • Trust erosion — people lose confidence in digital services

Common Pitfalls

Virus ≠ All Malware

"Virus" is often used casually to mean any malware, but it's a specific type. A worm is NOT a virus (worms self-replicate, viruses need a host program).

Tasks

Remember

Name 5 types of malware and explain how each one works.

Understand

Explain the difference between a hacker and a cracker.

Analyze

A hospital's computer system is hit by ransomware. Analyze the ethical, financial, and safety problems this causes.

Self-Check Quiz

Q1: How does a worm differ from a virus?

A worm self-replicates across networks without needing a host program. A virus attaches to a program and spreads when that program runs.

Q2: What does ransomware do?

Encrypts the victim's files and demands a payment (ransom) for the decryption key to restore access.

Q3: Name 3 physical risks to computer systems.

Power surge/failure, fire/flood, hardware failure, theft, overheating.
